# Recruitment and Outreach

Turnout is the most common way community programs fail. The fix is almost always the same: personal invitations from trusted people, not flyers.

## Who to reach first

Design for the resident most likely to be *acted on* by automated systems, not the one most excited about AI:
- Seniors (the main target of voice-clone and phishing scams)
- Renters and people navigating housing problems
- People dealing with benefits, utilities, or city services
- Small business owners
- Neighborhood association and block club volunteers

If your first room is mostly tech enthusiasts, the outreach went through the wrong channels.

## Channels, in order of what works

1. **Community connectors.** Two or three trusted neighbors who each personally invite 8-10 people. Pay them a stipend if you can. This alone usually fills the room.
2. **Host organizations' own lists.** Church bulletins, senior center announcements, school parent groups, library event calendars, tenant associations.
3. **Neighborhood association meetings.** Five minutes on the agenda, a sign-up sheet, and the flyer.
4. **Places people already are.** Barbershops, laundromats, pharmacies, corner stores, bus stops near the venue.
5. **Online groups.** Neighborhood Facebook and Nextdoor groups, posted by a member rather than the organization.

Use the [invitation flyer](https://neighborhoodos.org/kit/#h-flyer) from the facilitator kit.

## A 30-second invitation (for connectors)

> “There’s a free evening at [place] on [day] about AI, the chatbots and the scams. It’s not techy. They explain what these things actually are, how people are getting scammed with fake voices, and what not to type into them. There’s food, and they pay you thirty dollars for your time. Want to come with me?”

The last line matters most. People come *with* someone.

## Announcement text (for bulletins and newsletters)

> **Free: AI, scams, and your privacy.** Three friendly evenings for neighbors at [place], starting [date], [time]. Learn what AI chatbots can and can’t do, how scammers are using fake voices and messages, and how to keep your information safe. Then bring a real problem from your block and leave with a plan. No experience needed. Food provided, and you’ll be paid $30 for each session you attend. Questions or accommodations: [contact].

## Removing barriers

- **Time:** offer evenings and one weekend option. Ask the first group what works.
- **Childcare:** a supervised kids’ table often doubles attendance for parents.
- **Language:** if a meaningful share of the neighborhood speaks another language, run a session in that language rather than only interpreting.
- **Transportation:** pick a venue on a bus line; offer ride coordination.
- **Devices:** say clearly “no device needed.” Bring loaners.
- **Trust:** name the host organization residents already know. Say plainly that nothing is recorded or sold.

## Keeping people coming back

- Send a short reminder the day before, from the connector who invited them, not the organization.
- Start each session by reporting back on something from the last one.
- In Solve clinics, report back on every problem, including the ones that didn’t move. Follow-through is what builds trust.
- Invite returning participants to help: greeting, co-facilitating a table, bringing a neighbor.
